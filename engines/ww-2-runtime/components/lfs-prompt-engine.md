## The 7 Principles

### 1. Hook = Compression
One specific detail — a number, a dollar amount, a named object — in the first sentence. The reader sees the problem before they understand it. Maximum 2 sentences before the story begins. Atmosphere kills hooks. Specificity stops scrolls.

When the per-task prompt includes a VERBATIM HOOK, sentence one is literal source copy: same wound noun, same number, same punctuation. Clause one names the thing the reader is suffering against. The scene can unfold from sentence two.

Near-miss: "I bleached my counters at 11. Three new droppings at 6."
Winner: "I found mouse droppings on the counter for the third morning in a row and I bleached it twice last night."

### 2. Failed Solutions = Dismantled Individually
Each failed solution gets its own paragraph. Name the product or approach. Validate it was reasonable. Then explain WHY it structurally could never have worked — the biological or mechanical reason it was always going to fail. A list of failures creates empathy but no insight. A dismantled belief creates a gap that only the mechanism can fill.

### 3. Mechanism = Discovery Arc
The reader arrives at the conclusion before you state it. Start from something they already accept as true. Chain cause-and-effect forward — 3-5 if/then links, each individually obvious — until the mechanism is the only possible conclusion. Truth structure, not trust structure. The mechanism is revealed like a secret the narrator uncovered, not explained like a textbook entry.

### 4. Search Struggle = Multi-Step Active Hunt
Between learning WHY and finding the product, the narrator SEARCHES. Multiple named channels (stores, websites, forums, professionals). Specific dead ends. Time invested. Emotional cost of the hunt. The product is the LAST thing found, not the first. The reader trusts a person who hunted, not a person who was handed an answer.

### 5. CTA = Story Close
The CTA emerges from the narrator's story, not from a landing page. Three beats in order: personal share (how they got it), peer cascade (who else asked about it), risk removal as a quiet conditional (guarantee framed as "worst case"). A permission beat precedes the close — the narrator gives the reader an out, which paradoxically increases action.

### 6. Lead Ingredients First
Name the 1-2 hero ingredients that make this product different from everything the reader already tried. These are the "missing piece" — the ingredients that retail products leave out. Supporting ingredients come second and are explicitly framed as the support layer, not the breakthrough.

### 7. Voice = One Person, One Moment
Every sentence the reader can SEE sells. Every sentence they can't see is dead weight. Anglo-Saxon words (short, blunt, physical) create images. Latin-derived words (significant, efficacious) create abstractions. The narrator is specific about dollars, brand names, and equipment. Understated about frustration. Periods only. Plain mechanical language — the way someone describes fixing a real problem to their neighbor.

## Additional Craft Rules

Every sentence earns the next one. Each line opens a question only the NEXT line answers. Tease, delay, promise between sections — the reader is always owed something they haven't received yet.

The reader is never the problem. The cause is hidden, untested, or missed by everyone selling them the wrong solution. The mechanism is an exoneration.

Describe what the product DOES, not what it IS. If the reader could replicate the solution without buying, you wrote too much education. 70% motivation, 30% education.

Sell the identity, not the product. Stack outcomes through four levels: product benefit → lifestyle change → emotional shift → identity transformation.

The required outline supplies the beat order. The selected LFS format supplies the narrator role, argument shape, and weight distribution. Your job is to turn that blueprint into copy a lazy reader can scan without effort.

Use `========` between major sections.

Put a blank line before and after every divider.

Write one thought per paragraph.

Put a blank line after every sentence.

Keep each paragraph to one sentence or one short fragment.

Use plain sentences instead of markdown headings, bold, italics, or commentary labels.
