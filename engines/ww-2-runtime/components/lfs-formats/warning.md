FORMAT: WARNING
Alarm-pulling urgency. The narrator is protecting the reader from something invisible that's getting worse every day they wait.


LFS V4.1 OUTLINE USE:
This file is the selected native shape for the outline writer. Treat the format as the container, then fill every beat with the source swipe pressure curve, product mechanism, and buyer-facing scene evidence.

The outline must preserve this format's narrator role, emotional engine, beat order, and proof weight. Each beat should name what gets said, what concrete scene or evidence carries it, where the mechanism enters, and where the product earns its first product mention.

Recovery proof, result timelines, victory scenes, and CTA confidence enter after the product or routine discovery has happened. The final script should be planned as feed-native LFS: one thought per paragraph, blank line between thoughts, `========` dividers between sections.

BEAT SEQUENCE:
1. Hook — a visible symptom that implies invisible damage underneath. A number that makes the scale feel urgent. "If you see [one visible thing], here's what's already happening underneath."
2. The invisible damage — what the reader can't see. Scale it: how fast it spreads, how much worse it gets per day/week, what the endpoint looks like if nothing changes.
3. Why waiting makes it worse — the acceleration. This isn't a stable problem. Every day of delay compounds the damage. Specific numbers or timelines.
4. What most people do — the common responses. Each one explained as reasonable but structurally unable to reach the real problem. Compressed — 1-2 sentences per failed approach, not full paragraphs.
5. Why those responses fail — one structural reason that covers all of them. The mechanism enters here as the thing they're all missing.
6. The mechanism — framed as the thing the narrator wishes someone had told them sooner. Urgency tone: "once you understand this, you can't unknow it."
7. The search + product — compressed. The narrator found it and acted fast. The urgency of the format carries into the discovery.
8. The result — framed as "caught it in time." The narrator's timeline vs what would have happened if they'd waited.
9. CTA — protective tone. "If you're seeing [symptom], act now." The urgency is real but not manufactured — it comes from the damage timeline established in beats 2-3.

WEIGHT: 40% on beats 2-3 (the invisible damage + acceleration). The warning format lives on making the reader feel the COST OF WAITING. If the urgency feels manufactured or generic, the format failed. It must be grounded in specific, believable damage timelines.

WHAT MAKES THIS DIFFERENT: The narrator is a protector, not a sufferer or investigator. They've already solved their problem — they're warning the reader because they almost didn't act in time. The emotional register is "I need you to hear this" not "let me tell you my story."
