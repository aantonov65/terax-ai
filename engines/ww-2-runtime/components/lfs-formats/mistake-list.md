FORMAT: MISTAKE LIST
Self-deprecating accountability. Each mistake is a counter-argument in disguise.


LFS V4.1 OUTLINE USE:
This file is the selected native shape for the outline writer. Treat the format as the container, then fill every beat with the source swipe pressure curve, product mechanism, and buyer-facing scene evidence.

The outline must preserve this format's narrator role, emotional engine, beat order, and proof weight. Each beat should name what gets said, what concrete scene or evidence carries it, where the mechanism enters, and where the product earns its first product mention.

Recovery proof, result timelines, victory scenes, and CTA confidence enter after the product or routine discovery has happened. The final script should be planned as feed-native LFS: one thought per paragraph, blank line between thoughts, `========` dividers between sections.

BEAT SEQUENCE:
1. Hook — the count + the admission. "[N] mistakes I made trying to [solve problem] (and the one thing that actually worked)." The number and the parenthetical create a double hook.
2. Mistake 1 — the most common thing everyone tries first. Name it, price it, explain what happened. The narrator owns it: "my fault for not reading the label."
3. Mistake 2 — the escalation. Something more expensive or time-consuming. The narrator invested more, got the same result.
4. Mistake 3 — the one they believed in longest. Emotional weight. "I really thought this one was going to work."
5. Mistake 4 (optional) — the professional option. "I finally broke down and called..." Follow the money.
6. The pattern — step back. What did all the mistakes have in common? One structural limitation. This is where the mechanism enters: "I wasn't wrong about what to do. I was wrong about what was actually causing it."
7. The mechanism — brief. Framed as the lesson the mistakes taught. Each mistake is retroactively explained by the mechanism.
8. The last mistake — finding the product. Framed as another gamble: "figured what's another $29 at this point." Low expectation. The narrator expected to fail again.
9. The result — the surprise. It worked. Framed as relief, not triumph. "I almost didn't try it."
10. CTA — humble tone. "Learn from my mistakes." The narrator saves the reader from the same expensive education.

WEIGHT: 50% on beats 2-5 (the mistakes). Each mistake must feel like its own mini-story with a specific dollar amount, timeframe, and failure moment. If the mistakes are generic ("I tried a few things"), the format failed.

WHAT MAKES THIS DIFFERENT: The narrator is self-deprecating, not authoritative. Each mistake is a confession. The reader feels "I did that too" or "I was about to do that." Trust is built through shared failure, not credentials. The mechanism is the LESSON, not the lecture.
