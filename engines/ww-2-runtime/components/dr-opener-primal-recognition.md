# DR Opener Principle — Primal Recognition

You are about to write the first sentence of a Facebook ad.

Picture the scene. The reader is on their phone in bed at 11:47 PM. They have already swiped past a recipe video, a friend's vacation photo, a debate clip from yesterday's news. Their thumb is moving on autopilot. Their attention is post-coffee, pre-sleep — half-engaged, fully scanning. You have less than half a second of their cognition before the next swipe.

In that half second, the only thing that can stop them is **primal recognition** — the literal word for the thing that is wrong with their life appearing in their visual field. Not a scene that suggests it. Not a metaphor that points at it. The word itself. The word their brain has been searching for at 3am on Reddit. The word they have typed into Google with the keyboard at half-tilt because they can't quite believe they're typing it. The word that names the wound.

Your first sentence either contains that word or it doesn't.

This file is about how to make sure it does.

---

## THE RULE

**Name the wound in keywords. First sentence. Always.**

The wound is the literal noun for the suffering — *mice*, *joint pain*, *thinning hair*, *belly fat*, *can't get hard*, *panic attacks*. The keyword is the exact form the reader uses when they search at 3am.

Lead with the keyword. Not with a scene. Not with a question. Not with a setup. The keyword.

## WHY

Scroll speed beats craft. The reader is not reading; they are pattern-matching. The brain looks for one thing on social feeds: signal that this content is about *me*. The fastest signal is the word for my problem. Everything else — the scene, the metaphor, the mood — is read after the brain has already decided to stay.

Lead with anything other than the wound and you lose the reader before craft has a chance to land.

## WHAT WE LOSE WHEN WE LEAD WITH SCENE

A scene-led opener is craft showing off. It says: *I am a writer. I will earn your attention with my prose.* That works in a New Yorker essay. It does not work in a feed. By the time the reader has parsed the scene enough to recognize the implied wound, they have already swiped.

Scene-leads convert. Just slower. And on cold traffic, slower means dead.

## CONTRASTIVE EXAMPLES — five niches, three grades each

Each pair shows PASS / PARTIAL / FAIL for the same angle in a different niche. Extract the differential.

### NICHE 1 — Weight loss (woman, 40s, postpartum)

**PASS:**
> I weigh fifteen pounds more than I did before my second pregnancy and the weight will not move.

**PARTIAL:**
> Three years after my second pregnancy, I am still the same weight I was the day I came home from the hospital.

**FAIL:**
> I sat in the dressing room of a department store last Tuesday and looked at my reflection and didn't recognize myself.

(*Pass names the wound — fifteen pounds, will not move. Partial implies the wound through duration. Fail leads with scene, makes the reader work for the recognition.*)

### NICHE 2 — Erectile dysfunction (man, 50s)

**PASS:**
> I cannot get hard the way I could three years ago and my wife has stopped trying to start things.

**PARTIAL:**
> Something changed in my marriage three years ago and neither one of us wanted to say it out loud.

**FAIL:**
> I was lying awake at 4am last week, staring at the ceiling, replaying a conversation that didn't happen and I think still won't.

(*Pass names the wound directly with peer-voice phrasing. Partial dances around. Fail is pure scene — beautiful, useless on a feed.*)

### NICHE 3 — Hair loss (woman, 60s)

**PASS:**
> The drain in my shower fills with my hair every morning and I am sixty-two years old.

**PARTIAL:**
> My granddaughter brushed my hair last Sunday and stopped halfway through.

**FAIL:**
> There is a particular kind of grief that women carry that nobody talks about because it does not look like grief from the outside.

(*Pass names the wound + the dignity threat. Partial uses a witness — almost works because the reader infers fast. Fail is essay voice; the reader is gone.*)

### NICHE 4 — Joint pain (man, 65)

**PASS:**
> My knees crack when I stand up from the couch and I cannot finish a round of golf without limping by hole twelve.

**PARTIAL:**
> Last weekend my grandson asked if I wanted to throw a football and I said yes and then I said no.

**FAIL:**
> Aging is something nobody prepares you for, and the parts that surprise you are not the parts you expect.

(*Pass names two wounds (cracking, limping) and one identity threat (golf). Partial names a moment that implies the wound. Fail is wisdom-voice — the worst genre on a feed.*)

### NICHE 5 — Panic / anxiety (woman, 30s)

**PASS:**
> I have had three panic attacks in the last two weeks and one of them was in the parking lot of my children's school.

**PARTIAL:**
> I keep getting that feeling — the one I had in college that I thought I had grown out of by now.

**FAIL:**
> The body remembers what the mind forgets, and the body has been trying to tell me something for a long time now.

(*Pass names the wound (panic attacks) + count + location. Partial leans on shared experience but doesn't name. Fail is therapy-Twitter; converts on warm traffic, dies on cold.*)

---

## RUBRIC — does this opener pass?

A passing first sentence does ALL of these:

1. Contains the **literal noun** for the wound (mice, hair, hard, knees, panic, fat, droppings)
2. Contains a **specific number, count, or named location** (fifteen pounds, three years, hole twelve, the parking lot, six in one weekend)
3. Reads like a **peer at a kitchen table**, not a writer at a desk
4. Lands the wound in **clause one**, not clause two

A failing first sentence does any of these:

1. Opens with a scene, a question, or wisdom-voice
2. Names the wound only by metaphor or implication
3. Asks the reader to wait for the punchline
4. Sounds like an essay introduction

---

## WHEN THE RULE IS OVERRIDDEN — narrow exceptions

The rule holds for cold traffic. Two cases relax it:

**Override 1 — Warm-traffic retargeting.** When the reader has already engaged with the brand or seen prior creative, recognition is pre-loaded. The opener can lead with a question, a witness moment, or a punchline. The brand is the dogwhistle, not the keyword.

**Override 2 — Audience that has searched the keyword to death.** Some niches have keyword fatigue (postpartum weight, low T, joint cracking). The reader has seen the keyword 400 times this week. A counter-frame opener that *deliberately withholds* the keyword can spike attention by violating expectation. This is rare. Default is keyword-first.

If you are not certain you are in an override case, write keyword-first.

---

## OPERATOR CHECKLIST — six yes/no questions

Before the opener ships, answer these:

1. Is the literal wound noun in clause one?
2. Is there a specific number, count, or location in the sentence?
3. Would a friend at a kitchen table say it this way?
4. If a stranger read only this sentence, would they know what the ad is selling against?
5. Have you avoided opening with a scene, a question, or a metaphor?
6. Is the sentence under 25 words?

Six yeses = ship. Any no = rewrite.

---

## WRITE NOW

Open the briefing. Find the wound. Name it. Add a number or a location. Make it sound like a person. Stop at the period.

The first sentence is either the dogwhistle or it is the swipe.

## DR VOLTAGE OVERRIDE

This principle exists to stop vague, pretty openers from dying in cold traffic.

It does not exist to make the opener timid.

When the angle contains betrayal, divorce, sex, cheating evidence, public humiliation, money waste, body shame, or a witness moment, the opener must keep that charge alive.

A 31-word opener that says the wound in clause one and keeps the scandal is stronger than a 19-word opener that sounds like a symptom checklist.

Do not rewrite scandal into beige problem copy.

The wound noun gets her to stop.

The scandal gets her to read.
