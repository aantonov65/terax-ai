# META Headlines for Long Form Statics

## What the Headline Does

The headline sits below the image on META. It's 45 characters max. The user has already stopped scrolling (the image did that). Now they're deciding whether to read the body copy. The headline's only job is to make them start reading.

It is NOT a summary. It is NOT a tagline. It is a reason to keep going.

---

## The Rules

### 1. Say What It's About in the First 3 Words

If someone reads only the first 3 words, they should know the topic. "Her hair..." = hair. "$800 on hair..." = hair + money wasted. "Blood tests miss..." = medical + something wrong.

Never make them guess. Vague headlines die in the feed.

**BAD:** "She Gave Up on Everything. Then This." (gave up on what?)
**GOOD:** "She Accepted Hair Loss. Then Found This."

**BAD:** "Wind Used to Be Her Enemy. Not Anymore." (enemy how?)
**GOOD:** "Her Thin Spots Used to Show in the Wind."

### 2. 45 Characters Max

META truncates after ~45 characters on mobile. Every character counts. Cut articles (a, the, an). Cut filler words. Periods work as separators — they're shorter than "and" or "because."

### 3. Match the Copy's Entry Point, Not the Mechanism

The headline should match what the READER experiences in the first 2 seconds of reading — not the product, not the mechanism, not the CTA. If the copy opens with a hairdresser moment, the headline is about the hairdresser. If it opens with a drain check, the headline is about the drain.

### 4. One Thought Per Headline

Two thoughts = neither lands. Pick one.

**BAD:** "Her Hair Came Back and Her Sister's Did Too"
**GOOD:** "Her Sister Texted a Drain Photo. Clean."

## The 7 Headline Formulas That Work for LFS

### 1. The Story Moment
A specific, cinematic beat from the script. Reads like a scene, not a claim.

- "Your Hair Is Coming Back." — 3 Words.
- Her Hairdresser Saw the Regrowth First.
- First Hair Compliment in a Year. She Wept.

**Pattern:** [Specific moment]. [Reaction or payoff].

### 2. The Metric
A number that represents the transformation. Time, money, count, frequency.

- 3 Days Without Thinking About Her Hair.
- She Stopped Counting Strands at Week 3.
- Her Morning Went From 45 Min to 12.
- $800 on Hair Supplements. None Had This.

**Pattern:** [Specific number] + [what changed].

### 3. The Contrast Turn
Before → after in one line. The turn word does the work: "but", "then", period.

- She Accepted Hair Loss. Then Found This.
- Her Hair Isn't Perfect. But It's Hers Again.
- Not Thick Like 30. But It Stopped Falling Out.
- She Bought Wigs. Then Her Hair Grew Back.

**Pattern:** [Dark state]. [Turn word]. [New state].

### 4. The Authority Hijack
Positions the ad against the medical system or mainstream advice. Creates an information gap.

- Blood Tests Miss the Hair Glue Mineral.
- Your Labs Are Fine. Your Hair Still Falls Out.
- The Mineral That Holds Hair in Place.

**Pattern:** [Authority source] + [what they miss/don't know].

### 5. The Social Proof Beat
Someone else's result or reaction. Third-party validation.

- Her Sister Texted a Drain Photo. Clean.
- Two Sisters. Same Hair Loss. Same Fix.
- Her Sister's Hair Was Thinning. Back in WEEKS.

**Pattern:** [Who] + [their specific result].

### 6. The Direct Address
Speaks to the reader's identity or situation. "You" energy even without the word.

- Posting This for Women Losing Their Hair.
- If Your Hair Is Thinning After 55 Read This.
- No One Gets Meno-Hair Loss. Except Us.
- Only Women Losing Hair Understand This.

**Pattern:** [Identity callout] + [invitation to read].

### 7. The Behavioral Proof
Something the narrator STOPPED doing or STARTED doing. Behavioral change = believable change.

- She Stopped Checking Her Drain at Week 3.
- She Hid Her Thinning Hair for a Year.
- The Shower Drain Obsession Ended at Week 3.

**Pattern:** [Specific behavior] + [stopped/started] + [timeline].

## Writing the Second Headline (HL2)

Every copy gets two headlines. HL1 and HL2 test against each other in the ad set.

**Three approaches:**

1. **Slight variation** — same angle, different words
   - HL1: She Stopped Counting Strands at Week 3.
   - HL2: Week 3: She Stopped Checking for Hair Loss.

2. **Different formula, same angle** — switch from Story Moment to Metric, or from Contrast to Social Proof
   - HL1: She Accepted Hair Loss. Then Found This. (Contrast Turn)
   - HL2: She Bought Wigs. Then Her Hair Grew Back. (Contrast Turn but bigger swing)

3. **Specificity shift** — one is more specific than the other
   - HL1: Her Sister's Hair Was Thinning Too. Fixed. (compressed)
   - HL2: Her Sister's Hair Was Thinning. Back in WEEKS. (timeline added)

HL2 should never be a completely different angle. Same door, different knock.

## Common Mistakes

| Mistake | Why It Fails | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Too vague | Could be about anything — skincare, diet, therapy | Name the topic (hair, thinning, loss) in first 3 words |
| Too long | Gets truncated on mobile | Cut to 45 chars. Kill articles and filler |
| Selling the product | Reader doesn't care about the product yet | Sell the STORY or the INFORMATION GAP |
| Generic clickbait | "You Won't Believe What Happened" | Use specific details — names, numbers, timelines |
| Summary headline | "Woman Finds Mineral That Fixes Hair Loss" | Headlines tease, they don't summarize |
| Multiple thoughts | "Hair Came Back, Drain Is Clean, Sister Agrees" | One beat. One thought. One reason to read |
| No emotional charge | "A Mineral for Hair Health" | Add stakes — who, when, what changed, what failed |

---

## Quick Test

Before shipping a headline, ask:

1. If I read only the first 3 words, do I know what this is about?
2. Would I stop and read the body if I saw this under an image?
3. Is it under 45 characters?
4. Does it match the copy's opening energy?
5. Is it one thought, not two?

If any answer is no, rewrite.
